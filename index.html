Prologue

Across the market, companies are telling the same story in different words.

Some are pausing external design budgets to test what a small internal team with AI tools can do. Others plan to replace large external teams with a handful of in-house designers. Plans like these often change once they meet day-to-day reality, but they point in one direction.

This paper looks at that direction from the outside, in three steps.

What the evidence covers, and what it doesn't

Most studies on how companies buy design look at marketing and creative work, and several surveys lean towards US software companies. Our clients build physical and digital products in Europe. We read these studies as early signals: creative work moved first, and product design is following. Most survey fieldwork took place between mid-2025 and mid-2026.

Why act now if Europe is behind? We expect the shift to reach our sectors later and unevenly. If that holds, the gap is our window to build the offers before our clients need them. It is an assumption, and one we should test.

Force 01

AI is everywhere. Its value isn't, yet.

What's happening

Almost every large organisation now uses AI somewhere, but only about a third have started to scale it. Far fewer have changed how work is done around it, and those that have are the most likely to see value. Europe is earlier on the curve, and small companies most of all: 17% use AI, against 55% of large ones.

Force 04

Design is bought differently

What's happening

Production is moving in-house and outside spend is getting selective. The clearest evidence comes from marketing and creative work, which moved first; product design is following. The largest agency groups are cutting and merging: Omnicom cut more than 4,000 jobs after taking over IPG, according to the Financial Times. Buyers expect to pay for outcomes rather than hours.

Taken together

Design isn't shrinking. It is moving closer to the business, into smaller teams that are asked to do more, faster, under stricter rules.

Designers feel it. In Europe, the most cautious region, 59% believe the job market has declined and only 38% report rising job satisfaction. Figma's reading is worth keeping: the profession is “in flux, but not in decline”.

Team Transition & Leadership

The problem

A company replaces a large external design team with three to five in-house designers. Knowledge leaves with the old team, and nobody sets direction for the new one.

What we do

We help shape the new team, work alongside it for three to six months, then stay on as its part-time design lead.

What the client gets

A small team that delivers steadily within months, with nothing lost in the move.

For the people who use it

The product keeps improving through the change.

02

Specialist Bench

The problem

A team of four can't hire a researcher, an accessibility expert and a design engineer. Yet those skills decide whether the product works.

What we do

We bring in our specialists for a defined project, such as a research study or an accessibility review, and leave what they learn with the team.

What the client gets

Expertise when it's needed, without a hire.

For the people who use it

Research and accessibility reach the product itself.

Need 02

Build it right

AI makes building fast. Keeping products consistent, accessible and trustworthy now needs an owner. Builds on: consistency is the bottleneck; quality needs an owner.

03

AI Design Ops & Systems

One design system for every screen

The problem

The team and its AI tools build fast, but screens end up looking and working differently on every device and brand.

What we do

We build one design system that works across device screens, apps and web, written so AI tools can read it and build from it (appendix B), and keep it running with the team.

What the client gets

Fewer one-off screens and faster releases, from a small team.

For the people who use it

The same task works the same way on every screen.

Start with a pilot where one set of design rules (colours, type, spacing) feeds two kinds of screen, such as a machine display and a mobile app
04

Quality & Compliance

The problem

EU rules now require accessible products and clear labelling of AI, and more of the product is built with AI nobody fully trusts.

What we do

We review key flows against WCAG 2.2 AA, the accessibility level the EU standard asks for, and test them with real users, fix issues where they start, in the components, and keep checking.

What the client gets

A product everyone can use, and the evidence to show it.

For the people who use it

People with disabilities, and everyone else, can use the product.

Start with a 1–2 week review

Appendix F
How we use AI ourselves

The same standards we ask of clients

Nacar has no written rules yet for using AI with client work. Clients will ask, so we propose a first set.

01
Client data stays where the client allows

We use client material only in AI tools the client has approved, and never to train models.

02
A person approves what reaches the client

Every AI-assisted output is reviewed by a named person before it is shared.

03
We say where AI was used

Clients know which parts of the work AI helped with.

04
Research data is personal data

Interview recordings and participant data go into AI tools only with participants' consent and after anonymisation, in line with GDPR.

05
Someone owns the rules

A named owner keeps them up to date as tools and the AI Act evolve.
